US removes Syria from state sponsor of terrorism list

AFP , Monday 24 Aug 2026 The United States on Monday delisted Syria as a state sponsor of terrorism, rescinding a decades-old designation that had severely impeded investment.

The governor of Syria's central bank hailed his country's removal from Washington's list of state sponsors of terrorism as a "historic step" on Monday.

"Ending Syria's designation as a state sponsor of terrorism is a historic step that restores the country to its natural place in the global economic system," Safwat Raslan said on X, adding the bank was "working to build a modern and reliable financial system".

The US Department of State also revoked its designation of al-Nusrah Front -- also known as Hay'at Tahrir al-Sham (HTS) -- as a global terrorist organisation.

The Treasury Department followed through with sanctions relief.

"Today's action will help foster additional investment in Syria to promote political and economic stability," Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said in a statement.

Treasury said the moves were "in line with President (Donald) Trump's promise to deliver sanctions relief to Syria."

But the agency stressed that its removal of restrictions on Syria "does not change Treasury's posture with regards to countering global terrorism and our commitment to hold bad actors in Syria accountable."

HTS was earlier known as Al-Nusra Front and was formerly the branch of Al-Qaeda in Syria. But it broke ties with the jihadist group in 2016 and sought to soften its image.

The United States had initially announced in July its intent to delist Syria as a state sponsor of terrorism, a move seen as a vote of confidence in leader Ahmed al-Sharaa.

The step came as Trump met on the sidelines of a NATO summit in Turkey with Sharaa, a former terrorist who has sought to recast himself as a unifying figure after the 2024 toppling of the Assad family, which ruled with an iron fist for a half-century.

Study| The political imaginary of the tech right: From freedom to innovate to the reshaping of power

Dina Shehata , Monday 24 Aug 2026 The rise of the Tech Right is not limited to the movement of a segment of Silicon Valley’s elite towards the political right. It reflects the emergence of a political imaginary that reconsiders established assumptions about the state, democracy and power.

Having long been associated in the public imagination with social openness, the free circulation of information and freedom from the control of centralised institutions, influential segments of the American technology elite have become more critical of government regulation, more sceptical about the ability of democracy and representative institutions to keep pace with accelerating change, and more willing to ally themselves with forces on the political right or embrace ideas calling for power to be concentrated in the hands of founders, engineers and those capable of building new technological systems.

This transformation did not arise from ideas alone. It was also connected to the changing position of technology companies within the economy, the state and society. Platforms, cloud infrastructure, data and computing power have evolved from economic tools into essential infrastructure on which communication, work, knowledge, security and public services depend.

The rapid rise of artificial intelligence and intensifying international competition over semiconductors, energy, and military and technological capabilities have also given private companies and laboratories an increasingly important role in determining the choices available to governments. At the same time, the slow pace of political institutions, declining trust in traditional elites and the growing sense of bureaucratic incapacity have reinforced the belief that entrepreneurs and engineers are better equipped than politicians to understand and prepare for the future.

Against this background, what may be described as the “Tech Right” has emerged. The term does not refer to a unified party or movement, nor does it encompass everyone working in the technology sector. It describes a diverse intellectual and political field whose currents share a determination to reconsider the relationship between technology and power.

The issue is no longer limited to companies demanding lower taxes or less regulation. It now extends to more fundamental questions: Who possesses the knowledge required to determine the direction of society? Should democratic institutions be allowed to slow technological development or subject it to priorities determined by the public? Does the ability to innovate and build give its possessors a special right to set the rules to which others are subject?

The currents of the Tech Right offer different answers to these questions. Some place their faith in accelerating innovation and markets as the path to abundance and to solving problems that politics has failed to address. Others call for the construction of new cities, networks or communities governed by the authority of their founders while allowing individuals the freedom to leave them.

Another current believes that the future requires a close alliance between the nation-state and technology companies to build industrial and military power and the capacity to compete internationally. This field therefore does not rest on a single political programme, but on a varied collection of conceptions of the state, democracy, sovereignty, freedom and the future of the social order.

The significance of these ideas lies in the fact that their proponents do not represent marginal intellectual groups content merely to criticise the existing order. Some of them own companies, platforms, capital and technological infrastructure and maintain direct relationships with centres of decision-making. The Tech Right consequently combines imagination with capability: it envisages new political and social systems while possessing the resources that make it possible to test some of their features or turn them into reality. The influence of these visions is not confined to the United States, because the platforms, systems, software and governance standards produced by technology centres spread to the rest of the world, carrying with them specific assumptions about efficiency, authority and accountability.

This study seeks to present an analytical map of this emerging field by locating it within the contemporary intellectual landscape, analysing its principal visions and the actors who articulate them, and explaining how some of its ideas have moved from marginal forums and writings into companies, institutions and centres of political power. The question connecting these different levels is this: When technology becomes an essential condition for organising society, who has the right to determine its direction and purposes, and who should be held accountable for the consequences?

First: A Hybrid Field: Where Does the Tech Right Stand on the Intellectual Map? Understanding the position of the Tech Right within the contemporary intellectual landscape requires more than treating it as a direct extension of conservatism or neoliberalism. It is a hybrid field formed by the convergence of diverse ideas and traditions, some belonging to the right and others originating outside it or remaining open to different political uses. Its most immediate roots lie in what became known as the “Californian Ideology,” which combined a counterculture opposed to centralised institutions, economic libertarianism and a belief that technology, markets and entrepreneurship could expand individual freedom in ways the state could not.

Cyberlibertarianism emerged from this environment, presenting the Internet as a decentralised space through which individuals could transcend existing borders, authorities and hierarchies. This vision carried a genuine emancipatory promise. At the same time, however, it elevated the entrepreneur and the engineer as figures capable of building new systems outside public institutions. One of the foundational ideas that would later pass into the Tech Right crystallised at this point: those who possess the ability to build and innovate possess knowledge of the future unavailable to politicians or bureaucrats, and this knowledge may entitle them to a special role in determining the direction of society. The Tech Right does not reject expertise or hierarchies of knowledge in themselves. It redefines expertise around the capacity to build, innovate and produce results, replacing the expert associated with the state or university with the engineer, investor and founder.

This field overlaps with neoliberalism in its confidence in markets and competition, its scepticism towards government regulation and its celebration of individual initiative. Some of its currents, however, go beyond reorganising the state according to market logic. They envisage private companies, platforms and networks creating spheres of private governance in which they establish their own rules, with the founder becoming the effective holder of authority within the system he has built. The market consequently ceases to be merely a mechanism for distributing resources within an existing political order and becomes a potential instrument for constructing alternative political and social systems.

Its relationship with the new American right is characterised by convergence without complete correspondence. The two currents meet in their rejection of liberal elites, criticism of the administrative state and opposition to progressive cultural movements. Yet the conservative right and post-liberalism generally seek to restore religious and moral traditions and the national community, while a significant part of the Tech Right celebrates radical change, the dismantling of inherited institutions and the construction of communities that may transcend national borders. Their relationship therefore represents a political alliance around shared opponents and short-term objectives more than a single intellectual vision of society.

Neoreaction and accelerationism form the most radical wing of this field. Thinkers such as Curtis Yarvin and Nick Land question political equality and the ability of democracy to govern complex societies, calling for more concentrated forms of power or for technological development to be liberated from the constraints that slow it down. Reducing the Tech Right to these ideas, however, would be misleading. Accelerationism also has left-wing origins and currents, while transhumanism, which places its faith in using technology to overcome the biological limitations of human beings, does not necessarily belong to the right.

The Tech Right therefore derives its unity neither from a single intellectual origin nor from a common political programme, but from the intersection between faith in technological progress and scepticism about democracy’s ability to direct it. Three principal visions emerge from this intersection: accelerating innovation and markets, building communities subject to the sovereignty of founders, and establishing a new alliance between the nation-state and technology companies.

Second: Acceleration and Abundance: When Innovation Takes Precedence Over Politics The first vision within the Tech Right begins from the diagnosis that Western societies suffer less from a shortage of knowledge or technical capabilities than from the loss of the ability to build. Regulatory constraints, fear of risk, slow-moving institutions and the power of interests benefiting from the status quo obstruct the construction of housing, infrastructure, energy sources, factories, medicines and technological systems capable of raising living standards.

From this standpoint, Marc Andreessen presented in his essay “It’s Time to Build,” and later in “The Techno-Optimist Manifesto,” a vision that regards technology, markets and entrepreneurship as the principal route to growth and abundance. It presents the “builder” as the opposite of the bureaucrat and regulator who knows how to object but not how to create.

The idea of acceleration has a more radical philosophical foundation in Nick Land’s writings, particularly The Dark Enlightenment. Land does not regard technology and capitalism as instruments for achieving ends determined by human beings, but as an accelerating process that produces new forms of intelligence and power and seeks by its nature to free itself from political and moral constraints. In this conception, democracy does not represent a means of directing technological development.

It is an obstacle that slows a movement exceeding humanity’s capacity to control it. Contemporary techno-optimists do not necessarily embrace Land’s explicit hostility towards democracy or his vision diminishing the centrality of human beings. They nevertheless share his scepticism about politics’ ability to direct technology and his belief that slowing development may be more dangerous than unleashing it.

This idea acquired a more optimistic form with the rise of artificial intelligence and the emergence of what is known as effective accelerationism, or e/acc, which calls for accelerating technological development instead of slowing it down out of caution over its risks. Its advocates believe that societies become more capable of solving their problems as their capacities in intelligence, energy, computing and production expand, and that confronting the risks created by technology requires greater innovation rather than the imposition of prior restrictions.

Not all supporters of this current belong to the right. Its manifestation within the Tech Right, however, links acceleration to resistance to government regulation and portrays restrictions on artificial intelligence as an attempt by existing institutions and elites to control the future and protect their positions.

This narrative rests on genuine shortcomings. Regulatory procedures can become a means of protecting large companies from competition, while exaggerated fear of risk can obstruct necessary solutions in energy, health and housing. Governments may also lack expertise or be governed by short-term electoral calculations. The slogan of “building” therefore possesses an evident appeal: it expresses a rejection of stagnation and restores value to material achievement.

Within this narrative, however, the innovator ceases to be merely the possessor of a freedom that should be protected. He becomes the bearer of advanced knowledge of what society needs, and his ability to build can develop into a claim that he is entitled to determine the direction of the future. Attracting investors and users is then presented as sufficient evidence of public acceptance, while politics arrives later to adapt itself to a reality that companies have already created.

Third: Founder Sovereignty: From Exit to the Network State If the ability to build gives the founder advanced knowledge and an exceptional contribution, some currents of the Tech Right take a further step by granting him extensive authority over the sphere he has constructed. This transition appears in the redefinition of freedom around the concepts of “exit” and “voice.” Democracy rests on those subject to rules possessing a voice in establishing and revising them. In the alternative conception, freedom is measured by the individual’s ability to leave a system with which he is dissatisfied and join another. Instead of reforming an institution from within, it is assumed that competition among cities, platforms and communities for residents and capital will improve governance, just as competition among companies improves goods and services.

This vision appears in projects that vary in ambition and in the degree to which they depart from democracy. In his essay “The Education of a Libertarian,” Peter Thiel expressed scepticism about the possibility of reconciling freedom and democracy and called for ways of escaping politics rather than attempting to reform it. He identified three domains in which technology could create new spaces of freedom: cyberspace, with its currencies and communities transcending state control; outer space as a future field of settlement; and seasteading, or the creation of independent floating communities allowing different social and legal systems to be tested.

Curtis Yarvin, one of the most prominent thinkers associated with neoreaction, proposes a more explicit model based on managing the state as a company led by a chief executive exercising unified authority, while individuals retain the right to leave if they reject the manner of government. Under this conception, authority derives its legitimacy from clear responsibility and the capacity to produce results, rather than from representation or equal participation.

Balaji Srinivasan presents a different formulation in his project of the “network state.” Such a state begins as a digital community formed around shared values or objectives. It organises its members and accumulates resources before acquiring territories dispersed across several states, eventually seeking political recognition. The project does not rest on taking control of an existing state.

It begins by founding a community in cyberspace and then converting its economic and social cohesion into a territorial and political presence. Territory therefore no longer creates the community; the community constructs itself through the network before seeking land and sovereignty.

These projects do not represent a single doctrine. Yarvin defends highly concentrated authority, while Srinivasan gives greater importance to voluntary association and bottom-up community-building. Thiel does not embrace one complete political model. They nevertheless intersect in what may be called “founder sovereignty”: the relocation of the centre of the political order from the citizen to the mobile individual; from the people as the possessors of constituent power to the founder as the builder of the community and its rules; and from participation in determining those rules to choosing among ready-made systems. Governance becomes a service offered by competing providers, while the individual is transformed from a citizen participating in the creation of the system into a user selecting the system most suited to him.

This conception draws attention to the fact that participation is not the only guarantee of freedom. The ability to leave may offer protection from closed systems or coercive majorities, while the availability of multiple models may expand the scope for experimentation and prevent the failure of a single system from being generalised.

The effective capacity to exit, however, is distributed extremely unequally. A person possessing wealth, skills and multiple passports can relocate more easily than someone whose work, family and rights are tied to one place. Initial consent to join also does not constitute a permanent mandate for the founder, particularly when data, assets and relationships accumulate within the entity and the cost of departure increases. The formal right to leave may then become a weak substitute for the ability to object and demand accountability.

More importantly, these projects do not arise entirely outside the state. A private city needs laws protecting its contracts and property, as well as security and infrastructure. A network state requires payment and communication systems and international recognition capable of turning digital membership into enforceable rights. Sovereignty therefore does not disappear. Some of its functions are redistributed in favour of private spheres subject to the authority of their founders. A fundamental paradox consequently emerges: the state portrayed as an obstacle to innovation remains a condition for protecting the projects that seek to transcend it. This paradox would lead another current within the Tech Right to abandon the dream of exit and instead seek a new alliance between the nation-state and technology companies.

Fourth: Techno-Nationalism: From Exiting the State to Allying with It

Not all currents of the Tech Right lead towards the search for independent spaces or network states. As international competition over semiconductors, energy, computing, artificial intelligence and military technology has intensified, a current has emerged which believes that innovation cannot guarantee national superiority without a strong state.

From this position arose “techno-nationalism,” which links technological progress to economic and military power and national sovereignty and calls for a new alliance between the state and companies to build the industrial and technological capabilities required for international competition.

Alexander Karp and Nicholas Zamiska’s book The Technological Republic clearly expresses this trend. It criticises Silicon Valley’s preoccupation with consumer, advertising and entertainment applications and calls on engineers and companies to restore their role in serving major national projects and renew the partnership that linked the American state with universities, laboratories and the industrial sector. Techno-nationalism enables the Tech Right to reconcile its hostility towards bureaucracy with its need for the state.

What is rejected is not the state itself, but the state that regulates, slows development, redistributes resources and subjects companies to restrictions. The desired state is one that finances research, purchases systems, provides energy and land, protects intellectual property, opens markets and supports national companies in international competition. It therefore differs from libertarianism, which places its faith in exiting the state, and from neoliberalism, which assigns the primary role to the market. It calls instead for a strategic state that mobilises resources and directs them towards specific industrial and military objectives. It also differs from traditional conservatism in celebrating change, innovation and the future more than tradition and stability.

This current corrects some of the assumptions of technological libertarianism by acknowledging that markets do not by themselves produce public goods, infrastructure or strategic capabilities, and that the rise of the technology sector itself depended on public research, military expenditure, education and law. It nevertheless leaves open the question of who defines the “national interest” within the alliance between the state and companies, and how secret contracts and military and technological systems are subjected to accountability.

Strengthening the state’s capacity and reducing its dependence on foreign sources do not necessarily expand citizens’ ability to direct it. Even so, techno-nationalism reveals that the Tech Right is no longer concerned solely with escaping politics. Some of its actors now seek to reshape the state from within, a transformation that prepares the way for its ideas to move from the periphery to the centres of power.

Fifth: From the Periphery to the Centres of Power The Tech Right is no longer merely a collection of writings and experiments circulating on the margins of American politics. Some of its leading figures have become part of the governing alliance, and its ideas have entered policymaking circles concerned with artificial intelligence, cryptocurrencies, energy, defence and government reform.

This does not mean that the US administration has adopted an integrated intellectual doctrine bearing this name. Rather, separate elements of its political imaginary have found their way into power: confidence in the founder, hostility towards bureaucracy, the prioritisation of speed and building, and the view of technological superiority as a condition of national power.

The formation of Donald Trump’s second administration in 2025 marked the clearest turning point in the movement of some of the Tech Right’s ideas towards the centres of power. David Sacks, an investor, former PayPal executive and one of the most prominent members of the network commonly known as the “PayPal Mafia,” became the White House Special Advisor for AI and Crypto. In March 2026, the White House announced the appointment of the first members of the President’s Council of Advisors on Science and Technology, to be co-chaired by Sacks and Michael Kratsios. Marc Andreessen was among its members, alongside several prominent technology-company leaders and investors. The rise of Vice President J. D. Vance represents another path in this transition. He entered politics from the world of venture capital and worked during that period for an investment firm co-founded by Peter Thiel, making him an important link between the populist right and a segment of the technology elite.

The effects of this alliance are more visible in policy than in ideological declarations. Winning the AI Race: America’s AI Action Plan placed the acceleration of innovation; the development of infrastructure required for artificial intelligence, including data centres, energy and semiconductor facilities; and the export of full-stack American AI technology packages to allies and partners at the heart of the strategy to maintain US leadership. At the same time, the Department of Government Efficiency, or DOGE, initiative, in whose initial phase Elon Musk played a prominent role, offered a model for state reform closer to corporate management methods. It relied on small teams operating within agencies, the recruitment of engineers, the modernisation of government software and systems, and the measurement of success through cost reduction and improved efficiency and productivity. The state was therefore no longer the antithesis of the entrepreneur, but a new sphere that he sought to redesign.

The alliance between the populist right and the Tech Right is nevertheless marked by contradictions. The nationalist right emphasises borders, national identity and the protection of the domestic market, while a segment of the technology elite emerged within global markets and networks that transcend borders.

Conservatives also celebrate tradition and stability, while techno-optimists favour rapid change and the transcendence of existing social and biological constraints. The alliance between them is therefore based more on common opponents — bureaucracy, liberal elites, regulation and progressive culture — than on a unified conception of the society that should replace the existing order.

The importance of this transformation lies neither in the Tech Right having achieved a final victory nor in its having become a unified movement. It lies in the success of some of its currents in converting economic and technological influence into a direct political presence, and in their movement from defending the freedom to innovate towards advancing a broader claim about who possesses the competence and legitimacy to lead society. At this point, the debate goes beyond the future of technology to become a debate about the future of government itself.

Kazakhstan's pro-government party wins parliamentary election, boosting president's grip on power

AP , Monday 24 Aug 2026 Kazakhstan’s pro-government Adilet Party won a landslide victory in a parliamentary election, officials announced Monday, a result expected to further strengthen President Kassym-Jomart Tokayev’s grip on power in Central Asia's largest nation.

Adilet, which is led by the former head of Tokayev’s presidential administration, won 71% of Sunday's vote, Kazakhstan’s central electoral commission said, according to preliminary results.

The second-place Auyl Party won 6.26% of the vote, with three other parties – the Nationwide Social Democratic Party, Respublica and Aq Jol – reaching the 5% threshold needed to win a place in parliament.

Election monitors for the Organization for Security and Cooperation in Europe said the vote was marred by a lack of distinct political opposition, with “all contesting parties supporting the incumbent president’s policies.”

Some 74% of the country’s 12.6 million eligible voters took part in the election, the central electoral commission said. Officials tried to boost turnout by having some voters travel to the polls by parachutes and hot air balloons.

The election was the first to choose lawmakers for the new single-chamber parliament, known as the Kurultai. It is the culmination of a constitutional overhaul initiated by Tokayev in March, when 87% of voters supported a referendum to merge the two chambers of parliament.

The vote also gave the president authority to appoint key officials, subject to lawmakers' approval.

Last month, Kazakhstan’s Constitutional Court ruled that the amendments effectively reset the clock on Tokayev’s previous terms in office, clearing the way for him to seek another seven-year term when his mandate expires in 2029.

The decision echoes those seen in several former Soviet republics, including Russia, Belarus, Uzbekistan and Tajikistan, where leaders have used new or amended constitutions to revise statutory term limits.

The 73-year-old Tokayev, a former Soviet official and Kazakh diplomat who previously worked at the United Nations, has been in office since 2019.

He has sought to maintain a balance between Moscow and the West since sanctions were imposed on Russia over the war in Ukraine. Tokayev has justified the constitutional changes by citing the need for rapid decision-making in a fast-changing world.

The election comes at a difficult economic moment for Kazakhstan, where inflation hit 10.2% in February and tax increases have fueled public discontent.

Analysts say the economic strain has made authorities wary of a repeat of the unrest of January 2022, when fuel price protests spiraled into violence that left dozens of protesters and police officers dead — a scenario Tokayev is seeking to guard against by concentrating power in his own hands.

The vote also took place against a backdrop of intensifying crackdowns on press freedom. Media organizations have urged Tokayev to drop criminal charges against several journalists under house arrest and to revise legislation to ensure freedom of speech. Authorities have opened roughly 10 criminal cases against independent journalists and popular bloggers.

El-Sisi reviews preparations for new academic year

Ahram Online , Monday 24 Aug 2026 President Abdel-Fattah El-Sisi met with senior ministers on Monday to discuss preparations for the 2026/2027 academic year, ordering officials to hold exhibitions selling school supplies at affordable prices before term starts.

The meeting in New Alamein City brought together Prime Minister Mostafa Madbouly, Education Minister Mohamed Abdel Latif and Higher Education Minister Abdelaziz Konsowa, according to the Egyptian presidency.

El-Sisi told the ministers to enforce the minimum wage for all school staff, including teachers, technicians and administrators, and called for tighter oversight of school fees to ease the financial burden on families.

The meeting also covered the rollout of the Egyptian Baccalaureate system, including exam rules designed to give students more flexibility and multiple chances to improve their grades. Abdel Latif said the Baccalaureate curricula have now been accredited by the International Baccalaureate Organization (IBO).

El-Sisi told the ministry to draw exam questions only from official textbooks and ministry-issued materials, and to give greater weight to Arabic, religious education and history, taught according to the ministry's curricula.

On higher education, Konsowa outlined plans for new international programmes, dual and joint degrees, and expanded partnerships with foreign universities, including efforts to open branches of overseas universities in Egypt. He also updated El-Sisi on plans to develop university hospitals.

El-Sisi called for measures to guarantee the quality of university education and hospital services, and urged universities and research centres to study the challenges facing the state and propose practical solutions. He also pushed for wider use of automation across universities to improve teaching quality and make assessment more objective.

Konsowa briefed the president separately on the status of university admissions for this year's high school graduates. El-Sisi called for training programmes that match the needs of the local and global labour market, along with initiatives to support talented and gifted students.

Konsowa also updated El-Sisi on preparations to host the 12th African University Games in Cairo from 8 to 16 September, an event the president said must go smoothly.

Israel bars two Swedish MEPs from Palestine West Bank visit

AFP , Monday 24 Aug 2026 Israel said Monday that it had refused entry to two left-wing Swedish deputies in the European Parliament who wanted to visit the occupied Palestinian territory of the West Bank.

Jonas Sjostedt and Hanna Gedin, members of Sweden's Left Party, were turned away Sunday at Israel's Ben Gurion Airport, the primary gateway for visitors to the Palestinian territory.

Israel's foreign ministry said the pair had "consistently acted against Israel", including by calling for international legal proceedings against Israelis.

"On various public occasions, they have spread false accusations against Israel, including allegations of genocide, deliberate starvation and the intentional targeting of children and medical personnel," the ministry claimed in a statement.

Israeli violence has reached unprecedented levels against the Palestinians. Israeli occupation forces and settlers carried out 2,256 attacks against Palestinians, their land and property in July alone, while it is expanding its military assaults and illegal settlements in the West Bank.

United Nations investigators charged in June that Israel was deliberately targeting Palestinian children in an ongoing genocide in Gaza.

Sjostedt, in a Facebook post, said that the two MEPs planned to meet human rights and charitable organisations, including Save the Children, and to see refugee camps.

"We were not going to meet the Palestinian Authority. The EU's and Sweden's embassies in Israel have protested against Israel's decision," they wrote.

Rawhi Fattouh, president of the Palestinian National Council, the West Bank-based legislative body, called on European parliaments to speak out against the exclusion of critical voices.

The denial of entry is "an attempt to impose silence by force and turn criticism of the occupation into an accusation, in clear disregard for international law and the moral immunity afforded to parliamentary work", he said.

Israel regularly allows in foreign lawmakers, but in a high-profile exception in 2019 it denied entry to two US Democratic congresswomen highly critical of Israel, Rashida Tlaib and Ilhan Omar.
